Understanding the metrics

What BLEU stands for

BLEU = BiLingual Evaluation Understudy. It's an automatic metric for judging how close a machine-translated ("candidate") sentence is to one or more human ("reference") translations.

Origin

Introduced in 2002 by Papineni, Roukos, Ward and Zhu at IBM, in the paper "BLEU: a Method for Automatic Evaluation of Machine Translation." It became the standard MT benchmark for two decades because it's fast, cheap, and correlates reasonably with human judgment at the corpus level.

Core idea

BLEU counts how many word sequences (n-grams) in the candidate also appear in the reference(s). More overlap → higher score. It checks both word choice (1-grams) and word order/fluency (2-, 3-, 4-grams).

The formula

BLEU = BP × exp( Σ (1/N) × log(pₙ) )

With N=4, this is equivalent to: BP × (p₁ × p₂ × p₃ × p₄)^(1/4) — the brevity penalty times the geometric mean of the four n-gram precisions.

n-gram precision (pₙ)

For each n (1 to 4), precision = (matched n-grams) / (total n-grams in candidate).

Clipping: a matched n-gram can't be counted more times than it appears in the reference. This stops a candidate from gaming the score by repeating a correct word many times.

Brevity penalty (BP)

Precision alone rewards short candidates (fewer n-grams = easier to get them all right). BP corrects this by penalizing translations shorter than the reference:

  • If candidate length > reference length → BP = 1 (no penalty)
  • If candidate length ≤ reference length → BP = e^(1 − r/c), where r = reference length, c = candidate length

Multiple references

When several correct references are given, BLEU takes the best (least penalized) match at each step — the candidate only needs to be close to one valid reference, not all of them.

Score range and rough interpretation

ScoreQuality
<10%Almost useless
10–19%Hard to get the gist
20–29%Gist is clear, but has significant errors
30–40%Understandable to good translation
40–50%High quality
50–60%Very high quality
>60%Often better/quality than a single human reference

These bands are commonly cited rules of thumb (Google's MT documentation), not a strict standard — always sentence/domain dependent.

Limitations

  • Only checks surface word overlap — no understanding of meaning, so correct synonyms or paraphrases get penalized.
  • Ignores grammar/semantics beyond n-gram order.
  • Sensitive to exactly how the reference is worded; more references reduce this issue.
  • Designed for corpus-level evaluation; single-sentence scores can be noisy or misleading.
  • Doesn't handle morphologically rich languages or non-whitespace-tokenized languages (e.g. Chinese, Japanese) well without special tokenization.

Common alternatives

  • METEOR — accounts for synonyms/stemming, better sentence-level correlation with humans.
  • chrF — character n-gram based, more robust across languages.
  • TER (Translation Edit Rate) — edit distance to reach the reference.
  • COMET / BLEURT — neural, embedding-based metrics with stronger human correlation.

In simple terms

Think of it like checking your answer against an answer key by counting how many words and short phrases line up, word-for-word.

Example

Reference: "The cat is sleeping on the sofa."
Candidate: "The cat is sleeping on the couch."

Every word matches except "sofa" vs "couch" — same meaning, different word. BLEU doesn't know they mean the same thing, so it counts that as a miss. 1-gram precision would be 5 out of 6 words matched (~83%), but any n-gram containing "couch" (2-gram, 3-gram) also fails to match, pulling the final score down further — even though a human would call this translation basically perfect.

What it is

A measure of how close two sentences are in meaning, using vector representations ("embeddings") produced by a neural encoder (e.g. Sentence-BERT, OpenAI/Google embedding models).

How it's calculated

Each sentence is converted to a vector. Similarity is the cosine of the angle between the two vectors:

cosθ = (A · B) / (‖A‖ ‖B‖)

Result ranges roughly 0 (unrelated meaning) to 1 (identical meaning). The diagram in this app draws that angle directly from the estimated score.

Interpretation

  • >0.8 — very similar meaning
  • 0.5–0.8 — moderately related
  • <0.5 — different meaning

Limitations

No grammar/fluency check — a scrambled sentence with the same words can still score high. Result depends heavily on which embedding model produced the vectors.

In simple terms

Imagine every sentence is plotted as a point on a giant "map of meaning." Sentences that mean similar things land close together, no matter what words they use. This metric just measures the distance between those two points.

Example

Sentence A: "The movie was fantastic."
Sentence B: "The film was great."

Not a single word matches exactly (fantastic ≠ great, movie ≠ film), so BLEU would score this near 0%. But the meaning is nearly identical, so their points on the "meaning map" sit very close together — Embedding Similarity would score this high, around 90%+.

What it is

Named after BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers). Introduced by Zhang et al., 2019 ("BERTScore: Evaluating Text Generation with BERT").

How it's calculated

Every candidate token and every reference token gets a contextual embedding. Each candidate token is matched to its most similar reference token (cosine similarity), and vice versa:

  • Precision — average best-match similarity, candidate → reference
  • Recall — average best-match similarity, reference → candidate
  • F1 — harmonic mean of precision and recall (the number usually reported)

Why it beats BLEU

Because it compares meaning-aware embeddings rather than exact strings, it correctly rewards synonyms and paraphrases that BLEU would penalize.

Limitations

Heavier to compute (needs a transformer model), still model-dependent, and less transparent than a simple n-gram count.

In simple terms

Like BLEU, but instead of demanding an exact word match, it's okay with a close synonym — it pairs up each word in your sentence with its nearest match in the correct sentence, and gives partial credit based on how close that pairing is.

Example

Reference: "The child is joyful."
Candidate: "The kid is happy."

BLEU sees zero exact word matches beyond "The" and "is" — a low score. BERTScore instead pairs "kid"↔"child" and "happy"↔"joyful," recognizes they're near-synonyms, and scores the sentence highly for meaning, even with completely different vocabulary.

What they are

COMET — Crosslingual Optimized Metric for Evaluation of Translation (Unbabel, 2020). BLEURT — BiLingual Evaluation Understudy with Representations from Transformers (Google, 2020).

How they're calculated

Both are neural networks trained directly on human quality ratings (not a fixed formula like BLEU). COMET typically uses source + candidate + reference through a multilingual transformer (XLM-R); BLEURT fine-tunes BERT on synthetic data plus real human WMT ratings. The output is a learned regression score approximating what a human rater would give.

Why they're preferred over BLEU today

Much higher correlation with actual human judgment, and they can weigh fluency and adequacy together rather than just word overlap.

Limitations

Black-box — no interpretable sub-scores like BLEU's n-gram table. Needs a trained model to run (can't be computed by hand). Different model versions can give different score scales.

In simple terms

Like asking a very experienced translation reviewer who has personally read and rated thousands of translations before — they've developed a "gut feel" for quality that goes beyond just matching words, and they'll notice things like awkward phrasing even if every word is technically correct.

Example

Reference: "She quickly finished her homework."
Candidate: "Homework her she finished quickly."

Every individual word is correct, so BLEU's 1-gram score would look fine. But the word order is broken English. COMET/BLEURT, having learned from real human ratings of fluency, would catch this and score it low — the same way a human reviewer instantly would.

What it is

Using a large language model itself, prompted with a rubric, to rate translation quality — rather than a fixed mathematical formula.

How it's calculated

The LLM is given the candidate, the reference, and a rubric (e.g. accuracy, fluency, terminology, completeness) and asked to score each dimension plus give reasoning. There's no formula — the score comes from the model's own learned judgment.

Advantages

Flexible and explainable — it can flag exactly why a translation is wrong (wrong tone, mistranslated term, awkward phrasing) in plain language, and rubrics can be customized per domain.

Limitations

Not deterministic — asking again can give a slightly different score. Can be swayed by how the prompt/rubric is worded. No independent ground-truth validation the way COMET/BLEURT have against human ratings.

In simple terms

Like handing both sentences to a bilingual friend and asking, "Does this translation actually say the same thing, and does it sound natural?" They can explain exactly what's wrong in plain language, not just give you a number.

Example

Reference: "Please submit the report by Friday."
Candidate: "Kindly send report on Friday, please submit."

An LLM judge would flag this as awkward and slightly repetitive ("submit"/"send" duplicated), note the meaning is mostly preserved but "by Friday" (deadline) became "on Friday" (a specific day) — a subtle but real meaning shift — and explain that in a sentence, something a plain overlap-counting metric like BLEU can't do.

What "machine translation" means

Software that automatically converts text from one language to another, without a human translator. This app's Translate button is one example of it in action.

How the technology evolved

Translation technology has gone through four major eras, each replacing the previous one's biggest weakness:

Rule-based (earliest)

Linguists manually wrote grammar rules and dictionaries: "if you see this pattern in French, output this pattern in English." Rigid and couldn't handle exceptions, slang, or ambiguity well.

Statistical MT (SMT)

Instead of hand-written rules, the system learned patterns automatically from millions of previously translated sentence pairs (e.g. parliament records, UN documents), picking the statistically most likely translation for each phrase.

Neural MT (NMT)

Replaced phrase-matching with a neural network: an encoder reads the whole source sentence into a numeric representation, and a decoder generates the target sentence from it, word by word — allowing far more natural, context-aware output.

How a modern encoder–decoder translator works

Transformers & today's LLM-based translation

The Transformer architecture (2017) replaced step-by-step reading with self-attention: every word looks at every other word in the sentence simultaneously, capturing long-range context much better and enabling parallel, faster training. This is the backbone of both dedicated translation engines (Google Translate, DeepL) and general-purpose LLMs like Claude and GPT.

The key difference: a dedicated NMT model is trained only to translate. An LLM like the one powering this app's Translate button was trained on huge amounts of general multilingual text and instructions — so it translates by understanding the request itself ("translate X to Y"), which also lets it handle tone, idioms, and informal text more flexibly, at the cost of being less specialized and occasionally less literal.

Pipeline in practice

  1. Tokenization — the sentence is split into sub-word pieces the model understands
  2. Embedding — each token becomes a numeric vector capturing its meaning/context
  3. Self-attention / encoding — the model builds a context-aware representation of the whole sentence
  4. Generation — the target-language tokens are produced one at a time, each informed by the full source context
  5. Detokenization — the output tokens are stitched back into readable text

Limitations to know

  • Can still mistranslate idioms, humor, or culturally specific references
  • LLM-based translators can occasionally "hallucinate" content not in the source
  • Low-resource languages (less training data available) translate less reliably
  • Formality, gender, and tone can be lost or guessed incorrectly without context
